How it works

What this calculator does

“Interest-free” installments aren’t automatically free. Paying later has value, because the cash you keep in the meantime can earn a return. This calculator adds up the installments in today’s money (their present value) using the return you could earn, and compares that with the cash price after the discount.

It also shows the break-even cash discount, the discount at which both options cost the same, and the implied annual rate of the discount: the return you’d need to earn to be indifferent. If you can reliably earn more than that rate, installments win; if not, take the cash discount. Inflation is shown separately as the real cost of the installments in today’s purchasing power.

The formula

Installment = price ÷ n; cash price = price × (1 − discount)
i = (1 + annual return)1/12 − 1
Value today of the installments = Σ installment ÷ (1 + i)k, for each payment month k
Better option = the lower of the cash price and the value today
Break-even discount = 1 − value today ÷ price

Worked example, step by step

A TV costs $1,200.00 in 12 interest-free installments, or 5% less if you pay cash. Your money could earn 8% a year, and the first installment is due in one month.

  1. Cash price = $1,200.00 × (1 − 5%) = $1,140.00; each installment = $100.00
  2. Monthly rate i = 1.081/12 − 1 = 0.6434%
  3. Value today of the 12 installments = Σ $100.00 ÷ (1 + i)k = $1,151.29
  4. Paying cash saves you $1,151.29 − $1,140.00 = $11.29 in today’s money.
  5. Break-even discount = 1 − $1,151.29 ÷ $1,200.00 = 4.06%. The 5% discount is above that, so cash wins. It equals an implied annual rate of 10.01%: you’d need to earn more than that for installments to come out ahead.

Frequently asked questions

Are interest-free installments really free?

The installment price has no interest, but paying later has value because your money can earn a return in the meantime. That’s why it’s worth comparing against the discounted cash price.

What return should I enter?

Use what you could realistically earn on that money: a savings account, a fixed-term deposit or a low-risk investment. If you have nowhere to put it, enter a low value or zero.

How does inflation matter?

With inflation, future installments weigh less in real terms. Where nominal interest rates are high, that effect is already inside the return you can earn, so the inflation field only shows the real cost of the installments.

What if there is no cash discount?

Then enter 0%. With any positive return, interest-free installments almost always win: you pay the same total but later, as long as you really keep the cash earning in the meantime.
